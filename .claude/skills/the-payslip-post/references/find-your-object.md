# Find Your Object: the hardest bone, worked

This is bone 3. The object. Your tribe's "payslip."

It's the bone the whole post hangs on, and it's the one that takes real thought. So if you're stuck on it, you're in the right place. Don't skip it, and don't settle for a weak one. A great object turns a polite-likes post into a books-meetings post. A weak object turns the whole thing back into a beige post at a spreadsheet row.

---

## What an object actually is

An object is the physical thing your tribe handles constantly, that all their pain lives inside.

Not a feeling. Not a concept. A *thing* you could photograph or build a picture of. The planner's tribe doesn't handle "financial frustration." They handle a payslip. Every month. They log in, download it, scan to the bottom-right, and feel their gut drop when the net figure has barely moved. That's the object: a real artefact, wrapped in a real ritual, fixed to a real moment.

The reason it works is in `why-it-works.md`, but the short version: the brain recognises a familiar object the way it recognises a familiar face, in under a fifth of a second, before any conscious reading happens. Years of feeling are already loaded into the thing. You don't describe the feeling. You hold up the object and let their own brain do the recognising.

---

## The method: how to find yours

Work through these questions about your tribe. The object usually falls out of one of them.

**1. What do they handle every week, on autopilot?** The thing they open, check, fill in, scroll, sign, count. The payslip. The bank app. The calendar. The inbox. The spreadsheet. The form. The ritual is the doorway to the object.

**2. What do they open and feel their gut drop?** Pain has a home. Where does the bad feeling physically live for your tribe? What screen, what document, what object are they looking at the moment the disappointment hits?

**3. What's the high-frequency version, not the big-event version?** The pay rise is annual. The payslip is monthly. The thinking about it is daily. Always reach for the thing they handle *often*, not the rare big event. The post's job is to surface in their head every time the object recurs, so pick the object that recurs the most. (This is literally the difference between 6 DMs and 56.)

**4. What's specific to THEM and would mean nothing to an outsider?** The best objects are gates. A non-member should look at it and feel nothing, rows and columns, noise. If your "object" is something everyone has (a phone, a coffee), it's not tribal enough. Push for the version only your tribe owns. Not "a bank balance" but "the business bank balance the morning after payroll runs."

**5. What brand names or systems live on it?** The named system is half the recognition. Employment Hero. CommBank. Xero. Epic. Salesforce. ESR. The right person's gut reacts to the *logo* before they read a word. If your object has a recognisable brand attached, name it.

**6. Have you felt it yourself, or watched a real client feel it?** The seed has to come from a real feeling a real person has actually had. If you can picture one specific client opening the thing and feeling the drop, you've got it. If you're guessing, keep digging.

## Worked objects across industries

Here's the same hunt, run across different tribes, so you can see the shape of a good object. Notice every one is a *thing*, wrapped in a *ritual*, that the tribe handles *often*, that an outsider wouldn't feel.

| Tribe (the people, not the niche) | Their payslip (the object) | The ritual / moment it lives inside |
|---|---|---|
| The high-earning PAYG specialist who did everything right | the payslip itself | logging in monthly, scanning to the bottom-right net figure |
| The agency founder doing $2M who pays everyone before themselves | the business bank balance | the number in the account the morning after payroll runs |
| The tradie a bookkeeper serves | the shoebox of receipts in the ute | every quarter, tipping it out on the kitchen table before BAS |
| The divorce lawyer's newly-single client | the spare room that's now an office | walking past the door that used to be the kid's room |
| The NHS / hospital consultant | the rota and the on-call screen | opening the roster and seeing another weekend gone |
| The freelance creative chasing late invoices | the unpaid invoice list in the accounting app | opening Xero on a Monday and seeing the same names still "overdue" |
| The new mum returning to work | the daycare invoice next to the payslip | doing the maths and realising the second income barely clears the childcare |
| The farmer a rural-services business serves | the weather app and the rain gauge | checking the radar every morning before anything else |
| The restaurant owner | the daily takings vs the wage roster | reconciling the till at midnight against what the shift cost |
| The sales rep on a clawback comp plan | the commission statement | the line item where a closed deal got clawed back |

Read down that column. Every object is small, specific, recurring, and felt. None of them is a feeling. None of them is a concept. Each one is a thing you could build a picture of.

## The strength test: is your object strong enough?

Run your object through this checklist before you build the post. A strong object is a yes to all five.

- [ ] **Handled often.** Do they touch it weekly or monthly, not once a year? (High-frequency objects surface your post again and again. Low-frequency ones surface it once and go quiet.)
- [ ] **Specific to the tribe.** Would an outsider look at it and feel nothing? If everyone has it, it's not a gate. Push for the version only your people own.
- [ ] **Carries the feeling.** Is the pain physically *inside* this object? When they open it, does the gut drop? If the object is neutral, it's the wrong object.
- [ ] **Photographable / buildable.** Could you make a picture of it? An object you can show beats one you can only describe. The post lands twice as hard with the artefact under it.
- [ ] **You've seen it land.** Have you, or a real client you can picture, actually felt the drop on this object? The seed has to be real.

If you got five yeses, that's your object. Build the post around it.

If you didn't, usually it's the "specific to the tribe" one or the "carries the feeling" one that fails. The fix is almost always to get *more specific*. Not "a bank balance" but "the business bank balance the morning after payroll." Not "emails" but "the same three overdue-invoice names that have sat in Xero for sixty days." Specificity is where the recognition lives. Keep narrowing until an outsider would trip over it and the right person would stop dead.

---

## If you genuinely can't find one

Sometimes the object isn't obvious, and that's fine. Two moves that shake it loose:

1. **Go to the moment of pain and look around.** Don't ask "what's their object." Ask "when does my tribe feel the bad feeling, and what are they physically holding or looking at when it hits?" The object is almost always in their hands at that moment.

2. **Ask a real client.** "What's the thing you open or check that makes your heart sink a bit?" People will hand you the object in their own words. Then you've got the object *and* the argot, both extracted, not invented.

Don't write the post until you've got it. This is the bone that decides whether the right person thinks "that's me" or just scrolls past. It's worth the extra ten minutes.
