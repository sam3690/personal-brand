# The Stencil: the 7 bones in full

This is the core teaching doc. It's the skeleton of the post that pulled 56 inbound and 10 booked calls off a brand-new account, pulled apart bone by bone.

For each bone you get three things:
- **What it is**: the move itself.
- **Why it works**: the mechanism underneath. (Don't skip this. If you can't see *why* a bone works, you can only copy it, and copying gets you a tribute act.)
- **The line**: the real example from the planner's post, and beside it the re-colour for the property CEO, so you watch the same bone travel from one tribe to a completely different one.

First, here's the whole post, so you've got it in front of you. This is the anonymised planner version (a UK financial planner who sells to a specialist profession, we keep his name off it, the point is the bones).

> For [these specialists], a £10,000 pay rise might only put £2,400 in your pocket.
>
> The pay review body just announced a 3.5% rise.
>
> Whether you think it's enough is one conversation. What actually reaches your bank account is another.
>
> Because you know how this plays out. Logging in. Downloading your payslip and scanning to the bottom right. Gross pay is up. But the net pay figure has barely moved.
>
> For people earning between £100,000 and £125,140, this is what a pay rise can look like:
>
> → 60% income tax (the tapering of the Personal Allowance)
> → 12.5% pension contribution
> → 2% National Insurance
> → and sometimes 9% student loan
>
> That's up to 76p deducted from every extra pound earned in that band. On a £10,000 rise, that can leave £2,400 reaching your bank account.
>
> The pension contribution is real money, but it's not money you can touch until retirement. And even then, a penalty for taking it early.
>
> So the pay rise lands. Tax takes most of it. The pension locks the rest away.
>
> And now you're back, doing the extra hours, missing your kids' sports game, wondering what you're even taking home for all of it.

No hook formula you'd recognise from a swipe file. No "5 lessons I learned." A pay-rise announcement, a payslip, a stack of deductions, and one sentence about a missed sports game. That quiet little post pulled 56 of his exact clients into his inbox.

Now the bones.

---

## Bone 1: The logline hook

**What it is.** One line that names the exact person plus the painful number. Not a curiosity hook. Not "I'll teach you the secret." Just a flat statement about the reader's own world, with a number in it that hurts.

**Why it works.** This is the movie trailer. One line and you've got the cast, the setting, the genre and the conflict. "For [these specialists]" isn't a label, it's a velvet rope, only the right person feels named by it. And the number isn't decoration. Losses loom about twice as large as equivalent gains in the human brain, so "£10,000 rise → only £2,400" doesn't land as a small disappointment. It lands as a wound. The hook isn't asking a question. It's opening a wound and showing the maths of it. Notice what it does *not* do: no positioning, no "here's the secret", no elevating the writer above the reader. It states a fact about the reader's world and lets the fact do the damage.

**The line.**
- *Planner:* "For [these specialists], a £10,000 pay rise might only put £2,400 in your pocket."
- *Property CEO:* "$250k PAYG is now a poor Aussie battler. A $10,000 pay rise clears about 4 grand."

Same bone. A specific person, a number that collapses, stated flat.

---

## Bone 2: The now-moment

**What it is.** A piece of timely news the whole tribe is processing right now, this fortnight. A pay review, a new tax switching on, a budget, a season everyone's in at once.

**Why it works.** This is the timing multiplier. When the whole tribe is already processing the same news at the same time, the feed's attention is tilted toward them, and anything you post lands harder. You don't create the charge. You land in it. The planner rode the 14-day window when the pay review body had *just* announced and the whole profession was reading the same headline that week. The property post rode a new super tax switching on in five days. Same post two weeks either side does a fraction of the reach.

There's a second job this bone does. The name of the news ("DDRB", "Division 296") is usually an insider word, a password only the tribe carries. That's doing double duty, the human recognises themselves, and the algorithm reads the dense in-group vocabulary and routes the post to exactly the right cluster of people.

**The line.**
- *Planner:* "The pay review body just announced a 3.5% rise."
- *Property CEO:* "And in 5 days it gets worse. July 1, Division 296 switches on. Chalmers gets his hand into your super."

---

## Bone 3: The object (their payslip)

**What it is.** The artefact the tribe handles constantly, that all their pain lives inside. This is the hardest bone and the most important one. Everything else hangs on it.

**Why it works.** This is the move that turns a polite-likes post into a books-meetings post. The planner didn't *describe* their frustration in six paragraphs. He named the exact moment it lives inside: "Logging in. Downloading your payslip and scanning to the bottom right." The reader's brain processes their own payslip layout the way it processes a familiar face, same neural signature, so the recognition fires in under a fifth of a second, before they've read a conscious word. To an outsider that image is just rows and columns. To the tribe it's "holy shit, that's me." The object sorts his audience for him, automatically, before the body copy even gets read.

And there's a frequency trick hiding in here that's the difference between 6 DMs and 56. The pay rise is annual. The payslip is monthly. The *thinking about it*, the gut-drop when the net figure has barely moved, is near-daily. He anchored the post to the high-frequency artefact, not the low-frequency event. So his name now surfaces *inside* the moment, every single time the moment recurs. People don't remember frameworks. They remember moments. Name the moment and you get recalled during it.

This is the bone the whole skill protects. If you can't name your tribe's payslip, go to `find-your-object.md` and don't write the post until you've found it.

**The line.**
- *Planner:* "Logging in. Downloading your payslip and scanning to the bottom right."
- *Property CEO:* "Thursday, you open Employment Hero, scroll past the gross to the net, then check it against what hit the CommBank account."

Notice the re-colour swaps the *specific systems*, Employment Hero (the payroll system half of Australia gets paid through), CommBank (the bank logo they see when the money lands). Those named brands are recognition markers. The right person's gut reacts to the logo before they read the post.

## Bone 4: The deduction stack

**What it is.** The unsayable truth, said in arithmetic. A stack of line items, each on its own line, with whitespace around them, so the reader's eye tallies it up and arrives at the conclusion themselves.

**Why it works.** This is the bone that does the heaviest cultural lifting, and it's the safest way to say a dangerous thing. Here's why. Saying a thing out loud directly creates *common knowledge*, once it's said, it's on the record, everyone knows everyone heard it, and in most tribes there's a taboo against saying it. Saying it in numbers creates *shared private knowledge*, everyone quietly knows everyone agrees, but nobody said it, so the social fiction stays intact.

The planner never writes "the system is screwing you." He never complains. He stacks numbers. The maths says what no specialist in his tribe can say at work without looking greedy, and because he said it through arithmetic instead of a grievance, the taboo stays unbroken. Every reader knows every other reader agrees. Nobody had to say it. That's why it gets shared instead of attacked. If you ever catch yourself wanting to write the indictment directly, stop. Stack the numbers. Let the reader arrive.

The exact numbers also carry trust. "£125,140" isn't a round number, it's the precise point where a tax allowance fully tapers out, an insider figure an outsider would never get right. Getting the number exactly right earns more trust in three digits than a paragraph of credentials would.

**The line.**
- *Planner:* "→ 60% income tax / → 12.5% pension contribution / → 2% National Insurance / → and sometimes 9% student loan. That's up to 76p deducted from every extra pound earned in that band."
- *Property CEO:* "→ 47c gone the second it's earned. Top marginal + Medicare levy. → Let your private cover lapse, the Levy Surcharge bolts on another 1.5%. → Crack $250k and Division 293 rips 15% out of your concessional super. → HECS still on the books? Indexed every June."

**One hard rule:** the numbers have to be real. A made-up figure is a forgery that gets torn apart in the comments and voids the whole post. Verify against the actual source before you post.

## Bone 5: The locked-away twist

**What it is.** The line that closes the escape hatch. After the deduction stack, there's a comforting thought the reader reaches for, "well, the pension bit is still *mine*, so it's not really lost." This bone shuts that door.

**Why it works.** Don't let the reader comfort themselves out of the pain. The naive read is that the money taken for pension/super is still theirs. So the planner corrects the belief: real money, yes, but locked, untouchable, penalised if you grab it early. He tightens the pain instead of releasing it. And the rhythm does work here, "the pay rise lands. Tax takes most of it. The pension locks the rest away." Three short beats, each one a door closing, leaving the reader standing in a room with no exit holding £2,400 of a £10,000 raise.

**The line.**
- *Planner:* "The pension contribution is real money, but it's not money you can touch until retirement. And even then, a penalty for taking it early. So the pay rise lands. Tax takes most of it. The pension locks the rest away."
- *Property CEO:* "And the super they took? Locked till you're 60. Now Division 296 stacks another 15% on the earnings of anything over $3m."

## Bone 6: The 10:32pm thought

**What it is.** The private thought they never say out loud. The one they have lying in bed and don't say to their partner, don't say at work, because the culture forbids it. You name it for them.

**Why it works.** This is the line that converts a like into a DM. The instant you name someone's unspoken thought, you're not relevant to them anymore, you're *inside their head*. Different category of feeling entirely. And here's the wild part: "missing your kids' sports game" doesn't fire a memory of a past missed game. It makes the reader's own brain manufacture a future one. Because it's *their* brain building the painful scene, they don't blame the post for the pain, they blame their situation. You get credit for naming the problem; the pain comes from inside their own head; the two fuse. That's the whole conversion engine. The post never asks anyone to do anything. The reader's own brain is doing the work and looking around for someone competent to take it to, and you, having just diagnosed it perfectly, are the obvious destination.

**The line.**
- *Planner:* "And now you're back, doing the extra hours, missing your kids' sports game, wondering what you're even taking home for all of it."
- *Property CEO:* "All while you're flogging yourself at work and missing the young lads' Saturday footy game."

---

## Bone 7: No CTA

**What it is.** Nothing. No "DM me." No "comment GUIDE." No "follow for more." Deliberate silence.

**Why it works.** This is the bit people can't believe. When you've named someone's pain that precisely, the call to action is *redundant*, they're already moving. Worse, a CTA would cheapen it. It snaps them out of the recognition and back into "oh, this is marketing." The planner let the silence do the asking, and 56 people reached for the connect button on their own. The silence asks louder than "DM me" ever could.

**The line.**
- *Planner:* (ends on the 10:32pm thought. Nothing after.)
- *Property CEO:* (ends on "Advance Australia Fair." A bitter sign-off, not a CTA.)

## The shape, in one screen

```
1. Logline hook        →  the exact person + the painful number
2. Now-moment          →  the news the tribe is processing this fortnight
3. The object          →  point at their payslip; let their brain recognise it
4. Deduction stack     →  the unsayable truth, said in maths, stacked with air
5. Locked-away twist    →  close the "but it's still mine" escape hatch
6. The 10:32pm thought    →  name the private thought, in their words
7. No CTA              →  silence. let them reach for the button themselves
```

The bones stay. The colours change entirely: the country, the tax names, the bank, the swear words, the object, the exact thought. That's the whole method. The shape is universal. The paint is yours.
