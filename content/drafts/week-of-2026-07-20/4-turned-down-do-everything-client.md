I turned down a client last month who wanted an AI agent that could "do everything": qualify leads, answer support questions, update the CRM, draft proposals, one bot, one brain, every job.

Four years building backend systems for healthcare orgs taught me why that's the wrong ask. A system that does everything has no boundary where you can say "this part failed, that part didn't." When it breaks, and it will, you can't tell which of the five jobs broke or why, you just get a bot that's gone quiet or a bot that's confidently wrong.

I told them what I'd actually build: one narrow workflow that does the lead-qualification job, nothing else, with a clear success state (lead scored and routed) and a clear failure state (flagged for a human, logged, alerted). Then a second, separate workflow for the CRM updates. Then a third for proposals, once the first two were proven in production.

They pushed back at first. Wanted the one-agent pitch. I walked them through what happens when a five-job agent misfires on job three during a client's biggest week, versus what happens when one narrow workflow misfires and everything else keeps running.

They took the narrow version. Three months in, the lead-qualification workflow has never gone down. The CRM workflow got rebuilt once after a field-mapping bug, and it stayed contained to that one job the whole time.

"Do everything" is a demo pitch. Narrow and monitored is what survives contact with a real client's Tuesday.

Where's the line for you between one capable agent and a system you can actually debug at 2am?
